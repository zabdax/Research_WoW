# Outcomes, Novelty Case, and Publication Strategy

**If Phases 0–3 of the combined Idea 1 + Idea 3 project are completed as scoped, here is what you'll actually walk away with, why it's a genuine contribution rather than a repackaging exercise, and exactly how to package and route it.**

---

## 1. What You'll Actually Have In Hand

Concretely, at the end of this project, you and your teammate will hold:

1. **A five-hypothesis posterior odds table** — RFI/instrumental, cometary, hydrogen-cloud/maser, artificial power beam, and stochastic ETI beacon, each with quantified posterior probability and credible intervals, computed from a single shared Bayesian framework rather than five separate ad hoc arguments (which is all the current literature offers).
2. **A validated, reusable computational pipeline** — code that reproduces Kipping & Gray's (2022) narrower result as an internal sanity check, then extends it. This pipeline itself is a reusable artifact: anyone assessing a *future* SETI signal-of-interest could, in principle, drop new parameters into your framework.
3. **A sensitivity/robustness analysis** showing which of your conclusions hold up across a reasonable range of prior assumptions and which don't — this is often more valuable to the field than the headline numbers, since it tells other researchers exactly where the genuine uncertainty lives.
4. **A structured, scored census dataset** of Wow!-like events (the original signal, Méndez's 2024 mini-Wow detections, BLC1, and any others you add) rated on Sheikh's (2020) nine axes of merit — a citable dataset in its own right, independent of your Bayesian conclusions.
5. **A working qualitative-to-quantitative ambiguity conversion methodology** (turning Sheikh's "ambiguity axis" into Lingam et al.'s ξ ratio) — this is the one genuinely original methodological piece not sitting off-the-shelf in the literature, and it's transferable to any future technosignature evaluation, not just this one signal.
6. **A public, DOI-archived GitHub/Zenodo repository** with code, data, and documentation — mirroring exactly what you already did with STRATA, so this is a familiar final step, not a new skill to learn.
7. **A complete literature review** (already largely drafted from our earlier sessions) that is current through the September 2025 Benford rebuttal and therefore more up to date than any existing published review on the topic.
8. **One or more submitted/published manuscripts** — see the packaging strategy below for how to split this into the right pieces for the right venues.

## 2. The Novelty Case — Stated Precisely

When you write your introduction, cover letter, or talk about this project to anyone, these are the specific, defensible novelty claims to lead with. Vague claims like "new analysis of the Wow! Signal" get ignored by editors; specific claims like the ones below get read.

- **"First explicit multi-hypothesis Bayesian model comparison of Wow! Signal explanations."** Kipping & Gray (2022) tested one hypothesis (stochastic beacon) against non-detection data. Nobody has put RFI, comet, hydrogen-cloud/maser, power-beam, and ETI-beacon hypotheses into a single comparative framework with shared priors and a common evidence base.
- **"First application chaining Sheikh's (2020) technosignature taxonomy directly into Lingam et al.'s (2023) Bayesian ambiguity formalism."** These two frameworks were published separately, three years apart, by non-overlapping author groups, for general technosignature theory. Nobody has explicitly operationalized the connection between them, let alone applied it to a specific historical case.
- **"First hypothesis-comparison framework to formally incorporate the August 2025 Arecibo Wow! II revised parameters."** Every review or analysis published before that date is now working from superseded flux, position, and frequency values. Yours won't be.
- **"A methodological correction to a live disagreement in the field."** Benford's own observation — that non-detection campaigns constrain rarity, not which hypothesis is correct — hasn't yet been formally built into a probabilistic framework as a *shared nuisance parameter*. Doing so explicitly is a real methodological contribution to how the field should be reasoning about this whole class of one-off signals, not just about Wow! specifically.
- **"A reusable template for future signal-of-interest evaluation."** Because your pipeline and your ambiguity-scoring method aren't Wow!-specific in their mechanics, you can honestly frame the paper as offering the field a template applicable to the *next* BLC1-style candidate, not just a closed analysis of a 49-year-old event.

That last point is worth emphasizing in your framing generally: **you are not just solving a historical puzzle, you're building and validating a method.** That reframing alone tends to make reviewers treat a paper as more substantial.

## 3. Packaging Strategy: Don't Write One Paper — Write a Small Portfolio

Given the shape of what you'll produce, splitting the output into two or three linked pieces, rather than cramming everything into one submission, plays to your advantage. This mirrors exactly what the papers you're building on did (Kipping & Gray, the two Méndez Arecibo papers, Benford's two notes) — this field moves in short, focused pieces plus fuller treatments, not single mega-papers.

### Piece 1 — arXiv preprint (do this immediately upon completion, regardless of journal target)
Standard practice for every paper cited in your review. Costs nothing, takes a day, establishes priority and a timestamp, and gets you into the community's citation graph immediately. Do this the moment Phase 3 write-up is done, before or in parallel with any journal submission.

### Piece 2 — Research Notes of the AAS (RNAAS): the fast, focused result
RNAAS is genuinely well-suited to your Bayesian model-comparison result specifically. Its actual constraints, confirmed from their current guidelines:
- **1,500 words maximum**, including title, headers, captions, and references, with 150 of those words reserved for the abstract.
- **Exactly one figure OR one table, never both.**
- **Not peer-reviewed** — moderated by an editor for appropriateness and format, and typically published within about 72 hours of submission if accepted.
- Fully citable, DOI-assigned, indexed in ADS, permanently archived.

**How to tailor for this venue:** write it as a terse, results-first note — skip almost all the historical narrative (RNAAS readers are already the specialist audience), lead with one sentence of context, state your method in two or three sentences citing Pillars 1–4, and spend the rest of your word budget on the result. Your one allowed table should be the five-hypothesis posterior-odds table — that's your single most citable object, and RNAAS is the fastest way to get a DOI on it. There's typically a modest publication charge for RNAAS (much lower than a full ApJ-family article) — check the current fee schedule on the AAS Journals site before submitting, since fee structures do get revised.

**Why do this at all if it's not peer-reviewed:** speed and priority. Given the live, active 2025–2026 Benford/Méndez back-and-forth, being the first citable, dated record of a specific quantitative claim matters more here than it would for a settled topic. A non-peer-reviewed note doesn't carry the same weight as a full paper, but it's real, citable, and fast — treat it as staking your claim while the fuller paper goes through review.

### Piece 3 — the full paper: literature review + census + Bayesian analysis + sensitivity analysis
This is your primary, complete scientific contribution — everything from Section 1 above, properly written up with full methodology, limitations, and discussion.

**Best-fit venue: International Journal of Astrobiology (Cambridge).** Confirmed current details worth knowing before you commit:
- IJA has been a **fully Gold Open Access journal since 2024** — every published article is freely available, funded via an article processing charge (APC).
- The standard APC is currently listed around **£2,045 / $3,255** — but critically, **Cambridge explicitly offers a Research4Life-based waiver for corresponding authors from low- and middle-income countries** (Bangladesh qualifies), separate from any institutional agreement, and the journal also grants **discretionary waivers** beyond that for authors not otherwise covered. You must request the waiver **at or before submission**, via their waiver request form — don't submit first and ask later.
- IJA already published Caballero (2022) on this exact topic, so there's direct editorial precedent for Wow!-Signal analyses in their scope, and their aims explicitly list "the search for extraterrestrial intelligence" as core coverage.
- Peer review is single-blind (reviewers know your identity, you don't know theirs).

**How to tailor for this venue:** this is where your full narrative belongs — historical background, the complete chronological review, the four methodological pillars stated explicitly and early, the census with its axes-of-merit scoring, the full Bayesian comparison, the sensitivity analysis, and an honest limitations section (small-N census, prior sensitivity, the deliberately-included comet hypothesis as a validation control). End the discussion section by flagging the archival search (old Idea 2) as concrete future work — this signals a research program, not a one-off, and sets up a natural sequel paper.

### Backup / alternative venue — Journal of the British Interplanetary Society (JBIS)
Worth keeping in your pocket, particularly if your results lean toward favoring the power-beam hypothesis or if IJA review feedback suggests a better fit elsewhere — JBIS has hosted both of Benford's notes on this exact topic, so there's a direct editorial home there too if the paper's center of gravity shifts toward the engineering/beaming side of the analysis.

## 4. Practical Submission Checklist

Handle these before you're up against a deadline, not during:

- **ORCID iDs** for both authors, if you don't already have them — increasingly expected by both AAS and Cambridge submission systems, and it's what ties your growing publication record (STRATA, this project, future work) together under one identity regardless of institutional affiliation.
- **Affiliation listing** — list yourselves accurately (e.g., independent researcher / your college affiliation); this is completely normal and won't block review at either venue.
- **Data and code availability statement** — link the GitHub/Zenodo repo directly in the manuscript; both venues favor this, and you already have the Zenodo workflow down from STRATA.
- **Cover letter** — you've done this before (STRATA); the core pitch to lead with is the Section 2 novelty claims above, stated in two or three sentences, not the whole background.
- **Waiver request** (IJA specifically) — submit the Research4Life/discretionary waiver form at the same time as your manuscript, not after.
- **Pre-empt the predictable reviewer pushback** — have ready, specific answers prepared (not just in your head, actually drafted) for: "why include the comet hypothesis at all" (answer: deliberate validation control, state this explicitly in-text), "aren't your priors subjective" (answer: point directly at your sensitivity analysis), and "isn't this just repackaging published numbers" (answer: point at the specific Sheikh→Lingam chaining and the reusable methodology, not just the Wow! Signal result).

## 5. Suggested Sequencing

1. Finish Phases 0–2 from the previous roadmap (foundational reading → census → Bayesian model).
2. As soon as Phase 2's validation check passes (your pipeline reproduces Kipping & Gray's ballpark numbers), **draft and submit the RNAAS note** — don't wait for the full paper to be polished, since RNAAS is meant for exactly this kind of fast, focused result and the two documents can be written largely in parallel.
3. Finish Phase 3 (full write-up, sensitivity analysis, limitations) at your own pace.
4. Post the arXiv preprint the moment the full manuscript is complete.
5. Submit to IJA (with waiver request attached) shortly after, referencing the already-published RNAAS note as an early result within the fuller paper — this is normal practice and actually strengthens the submission by showing the result has already survived initial public scrutiny.

This sequencing gets you a fast, real, citable output within weeks of finishing the core analysis, while the more substantial peer-reviewed paper takes its natural longer path through review — so you're not gambling the whole project's visibility on a single, slower submission.
